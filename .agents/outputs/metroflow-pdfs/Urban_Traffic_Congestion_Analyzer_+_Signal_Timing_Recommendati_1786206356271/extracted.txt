1. Problem Overview 
The PDF asks for an offline analytics tool that identifies congestion peaks and bottlenecks 
from historical traffic flow data and recommends signal timing adjustments or coordination 
suggestions. It is decision support only: no real-time control, no guaranteed improvement 
claims, and all simulations must clearly state assumptions. In practical terms, the task is to 
help traffic engineers decide how to retime signals before any field 
deployment.jetirAI-Domain-X-Challenge-2026-Problem-Statements.pdf 
2. What the Problem Is Asking 
The problem is asking you to analyze how traffic congestion changes by time of day and road 
segment, then use historical counts to suggest better signal timing. The expected output is 
not just a chart; it should identify where queues and delays are concentrated and explain why 
certain green split changes or coordination ideas might help. The solution should also 
include a before-vs-after style estimate using a simple simulation or queueing

6. Engineering Context 
From an engineering perspective, this is a signal timing and coordination problem, not just a 
data visualization problem. The key goal is to allocate limited green time across competing 
movements in a way that reduces delay and queue growth. That means understanding 
saturation flow, cycle length, split, offset, and progression across intersections.jetir 
7. Core Traffic Concepts 
Congestion 
Congestion happens when traffic demand exceeds the ability of the road or intersection to 
serve vehicles. It matters because delay, queueing, and unreliability rise quickly once 
demand approaches capacity. Example: an intersection approach with insufficient green 
time will accumulate a queue every cycle.jetir 
Bottleneck 
A bottleneck is the part of the network where flow is restricted more than elsewhere. It 
matters because the whole corridor can be affected by one constrained movement. Example: 
a short left-turn bay may block a through lane.jetir 
Queue Formation 
Queues form when arrivals exceed discharge for a period of time. It matters because queue 
growth is the clearest sign of inadequate capacity or poor timing. Illustration: if 20 vehicles 
arrive per minute but only 15 can be served, the queue grows by 5 per minute.jetir 
Delay

Delay is the extra time spent compared with free-flow or ideal movement. It matters because 
delay is the main performance indicator for intersections. Example: vehicles waiting at a red 
light experience control delay.jetir 
Throughput 
Throughput is the number of vehicles actually served in a given period. It matters because a 
good signal plan should increase served traffic or reduce delay without causing spillback. 
Illustration: more effective green allocation can raise throughput on a congested 
movement.jetir 
Capacity 
Capacity is the maximum flow that can pass under prevailing conditions. It matters because 
congestion begins when demand exceeds capacity. Example: an approach has a finite 
discharge rate even if demand is much higher.jetir 
8. Signal Timing Concepts 
Signalized Intersection 
A signalized intersection uses traffic lights to manage conflicting movements. It matters 
because signal timing determines how available capacity is shared. Example: one phase may 
serve north-south through traffic while another serves east-west turns.jetir 
Phase 
A phase is the set of movements that receive green together. It matters because phases divide 
the cycle into service windows. Example: protected left turns may need their own phase.jetir 
Green Time 
Green time is the period during which a movement is allowed to proceed. It matters because 
more green generally means more vehicles can be served. Example: increasing green for a 
heavy approach may reduce its queue.jetir 
Red Time 
Red time is the period during which a movement must stop. It matters because it directly 
contributes to delay and queue build-up. Example: a minor approach may wait through a 
long main-road green.jetir

Cycle Length 
Cycle length is the total time for one complete sequence of phases. It matters because it sets 
the overall rhythm of the intersection and affects delay and coordination. Example: a 
90-second cycle serves all phases once every 90 seconds.jetir 
Offset 
Offset is the time difference between the start of green at successive intersections. It matters 
because good offsets help vehicle platoons progress along a corridor with fewer stops. 
Example: if vehicles reach the next signal when it turns green, progression improves.jetir 
Coordination 
Coordination synchronizes multiple intersections so traffic can move more smoothly along a 
corridor. It matters because one intersection cannot be optimized in isolation if nearby 
signals are coupled. Example: coordinated signals can create a green band for a dominant 
travel direction.jetir 
Green Wave 
A green wave is a coordinated timing pattern that allows vehicles to pass through several 
signals with minimal stopping. It matters because it reduces stops and travel time on 
arterials. Example: a commuter corridor timed for peak-direction progression.jetir 
9. Static vs Adaptive Signals 
Static signals use fixed timings or time-of-day plans that only change periodically. Adaptive 
signals adjust timings based on real-time detector data and changing demand. This 
hackathon problem is closer to offline planning than live adaptive 
control.AI-Domain-X-Challenge-2026-Problem-Statements.pdfjetir 
10. Offline vs Online Optimization 
Offline optimization means analyzing historical data and recommending a timing plan for 
later use. Online optimization means the controller updates timings continuously as traffic 
changes, which is outside the problem scope. Offline is simpler, more transparent, and better 
suited to a hackathon MVP.AI-Domain-X-Challenge-2026-Problem-Statements.pdf

12. Common Limitations 
Traffic demand changes by hour, day, season, incidents, and land use, so a fixed plan can 
become outdated. Detector coverage is often incomplete, and many intersections rely on 
sparse or noisy data. Coordination also creates tradeoffs, because helping one movement can 
hurt another.jetir

14. Traffic Engineering Basics 
Saturation Flow 
Saturation flow is the maximum discharge rate during a sustained green. It matters because 
it is the basis for calculating how much traffic a phase can serve. Example: if a lane 
discharges 1,800 veh/h under green, that is its saturation flow.jetir 
Volume-to-Capacity Ratio 
The volume-to-capacity ratio compares demand to service capacity. It matters because a 
ratio near or above 1 indicates likely queue growth. Example: 900 veh/h demand over 1,800 
veh/h capacity gives a ratio of 0.5.jetir

18. Common Mistakes 
One common mistake is assuming more green automatically solves congestion. In reality, 
bad allocation can shift queues or worsen coordination. Another mistake is ignoring offsets 
and optimizing intersections independently. A third mistake is presenting simulation output 
as guaranteed improvement, which the PDF does not 
allow.AI-Domain-X-Challenge-2026-Problem-Statements.pdfjetir
